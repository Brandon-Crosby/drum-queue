The dembow drum pattern: how to program reggaeton drums

Almost every reggaeton record is built on one rhythm, the dembow. It's simple to program and endless to vary. Here's the core pattern on a 16-step grid and the variations that make it sound current.

Where it comes from

The rhythm takes its name from Shabba Ranks' 1990 dancehall track "Dem Bow". Its riddim was picked up and re-made by Panamanian and Puerto Rican producers, sped up, stripped down, and by the 2000s it had become the backbone of reggaeton. Underneath it is a pattern called the tresillo, a 3+3+2 feel that runs through Latin and Caribbean music.

The core pattern

One bar of 4/4, sixteen 16th-note steps, counted 1 e + a 2 e + a 3 e + a 4 e + a:

  • Kick: steps 1, 5, 9 and 13. Every quarter note, four on the floor.
  • Snare (or rim, or a tight clap): steps 4, 7, 12 and 15. That's the "a" of 1, the "+" of 2, the "a" of 3 and the "+" of 4.
  • Hats: 8th notes (steps 1, 3, 5... 15) for a classic feel, or 16ths with the off-steps quieter.

Play just the kick and snare and you'll hear the boom-ch-boom-chick that defines the genre. The snare pattern is the tresillo, played twice per bar: 3 steps, 3 steps, then 2.

Tempo

Most reggaeton sits between about 85 and 100 BPM, with a lot of modern perreo around 88–96. Faster dembow styles from the Dominican Republic run much higher, and some trap-leaning reggaeton uses half-time sections that feel slower.

Sounds

  • Kick: short and round, often with an 808 or sub following the bass line. The kick is felt more than heard.
  • Snare: the star of the pattern. Tight, bright and short: a snappy snare, a rim, a clap, or a layer of two. Classic records use a very recognizable dry, slightly pitched snare.
  • Percussion: timbales, bongos, congas, shakers, güiro and cowbell-like sounds fill the gaps, usually panned and quieter than the snare.
  • FX: reverse cymbals, sweeps and short vocal chops at the end of 4- or 8-bar phrases.

Variations that keep it moving

  • Drop the first snare on some bars, or double a snare on a 16th before it, for a fill.
  • Move the kick. Taking out the kick on step 9 or 13 in the bar before a drop makes the return hit harder.
  • Layer a second rhythm in the percussion: a bongo or timbale pattern answering the snare, or a shaker playing straight 16ths.
  • Strip back. Verses often run with just kick and a rim; the full pattern comes in on the hook.
  • Half-time breaks. A bar or two where the snare only hits on beat 3 makes the next dembow bar feel like a release.

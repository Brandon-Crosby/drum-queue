How to get a cleaner drum split

The model does the heavy lifting, but what you feed it matters. A few choices before you upload can make the difference between a usable kit and a muddy one.

Use the best file you can find

  • WAV or FLAC first. Lossless audio keeps everything the separation needs.
  • High-bitrate MP3 or AAC next (256–320 kbps). Usually fine.
  • Avoid low-bitrate files (128 kbps and below) and audio ripped from video. Lossy compression throws away high frequencies and smears transients, and hats, cymbals and the crack of a snare suffer most.
  • Don't upsample or "enhance" a bad file first. It can't put back what was removed, and the processing can add artifacts.

Prefer the un-mastered version if you have it

If it's your own song, upload the mix before mastering. Heavy limiting squashes drum transients into the rest of the mix and makes every hit a little blurrier. A mastered song still splits well, just not quite as cleanly.

What makes a song harder

  • Loud 808s on the kick. The two share a frequency range. More here.
  • Distorted or heavily saturated drum buses. Distortion spreads every drum across the spectrum.
  • Lots of reverb. A reverb tail belongs to every piece at once, so it gets shared between stems.
  • Drum sounds that aren't drums. A plucked synth with a sharp attack, a vocal chop or a guitar mute can be mistaken for percussion.
  • Constantly changing drums. If every section uses different sounds, each sound repeats less, and the kit maker has fewer hits to average.

What makes a song easier

  • A steady pattern that repeats for most of the song.
  • Drums that are prominent in the mix.
  • Sections where the drums play with less going on around them, like intros and breakdowns.

Name your file well

If you know the tempo, put it in the file name: "song name 128bpm.wav". The groove MIDI uses that tempo instead of estimating it, which avoids half-time and double-time mix-ups. Why that matters.

When a split comes out rough

  1. Check the mixer first. Solo each piece. Often one piece is rough and the rest are fine.
  2. Try another version of the song. An instrumental, a different master or a lossless copy can split much better.
  3. Use the cleanest repeats. In the kit, a sound that plays many times is usually cleaner than one that plays a few times.
  4. Clean up in your DAW. Gates, EQ and short fades fix most bleed. Here's how.
